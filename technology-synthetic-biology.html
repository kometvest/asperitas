Secondary Metabolites

Translating natural discoveries into engineered pathways for the scaled production of high-value secondary metabolites and novel small molecules.

Beyond Protein Engineering

While much of modern synthetic biology remains crowded around protein engineering and biologics, nature’s most potent therapeutics are often small molecules—specifically secondary metabolites. These compounds mediate ecological interactions, defense mechanisms, and survival in extreme environments.

Through our metabolic engineering and pathway reconstruction capabilities, Asperitas translates these complex natural molecules into scalable biomanufacturing processes, targeting applications across therapeutics, agriculture, and industrial biology.

Metabolic Engineering

Rewiring microbial hosts to biosynthesize complex natural products previously accessible only through environmentally destructive extraction.

Pathway Reconstruction

Identifying and transferring multi-gene biosynthetic clusters from rare non-model organisms into tractable production strains.

Analytical Chemistry

Utilizing high-resolution mass spectrometry and NMR to isolate and characterize novel chemical scaffolds from biological samples.

Co-Development Strategy

Case Study: Amphibian Alkaloids

Poison dart frogs (Dendrobatidae) possess highly complex lipophilic alkaloid cocktails in their skin glands. Over 800 distinct alkaloids have been identified across amphibian species. Unlike many venomous animals, these frogs do not synthesize the toxins entirely on their own; their toxicity is diet-dependent, acquired through the bioaccumulation of compounds from specific arthropod prey.

Notably, some species are capable of metabolic conversion. For example, the frog can metabolize dietary pumiliotoxin into the significantly more toxic allopumiliotoxin using a unique hydroxylase enzyme.

This represents a prime opportunity for synthetic biology. By identifying the genes responsible for this enzymatic conversion and mapping the ecological metadata (the specific diet and habitat), Asperitas can reconstruct these biosynthetic pathways in vitro. These naturally optimized small molecules hold immense potential as highly selective ion channel modulators for next-generation pain therapeutics and neurological treatments.
